Many complex designs, including box-pleated ones, are laid out on a **grid**: the edge of the sheet is divided into equal parts, and every important crease falls on those divisions. The number of divisions is usually written as "a 64 grid", "a 52 grid", and so on.

The single most useful number to know about a design is therefore the **size of one grid cell** on the sheet you plan to use.

## The formula

For a square sheet of edge length *S* divided into an *N* grid:

<p class="formula"><em>cell size</em> = <em>S</em> ÷ <em>N</em></p>

For a rectangle, apply it to each side. A rectangle with a grid that has the same ratio as its sides (for example 1:2 paper on a 64 × 128 grid) has square cells.

| [The Monk of Fire](/artworks/agniv-the-monk-of-fire) | 150 cm square | 64 | 150 ÷ 64 ≈ **2.34 cm** |
| [Sunstriker](/artworks/sunstriker) | 100 × 200 cm | 64 × 128 | 100 ÷ 64 = 200 ÷ 128 ≈ **1.56 cm** |
| [Shivaji Maharaj re-design](/projects/chhatrapati-shivaji-maharaj-redesign) (in progress) | 100 × 200 cm | 56 × 112 | 100 ÷ 56 ≈ **1.79 cm** |

Notice that *Sunstriker*'s cells are smaller than *Maryada Purushottam*'s even though its sheet is bigger: it has twice as many divisions along its long side.

## Why the cell size matters

A cell is the smallest unit the design is built from. Narrow flaps (fingers, plumes, weapon shafts) are often only one or two cells wide before shaping, and many layers of paper stack up inside them. When cells get very small, those layers become hard to fold accurately and the finished details become cramped.

So when a design calls for a finer grid, you have two choices: **a bigger sheet**, or **a thinner paper** that tolerates more layers in a small space, usually both.

## Why some grids are easier to fold than others

A **64 grid** can be folded entirely by repeated halving: 2 → 4 → 8 → 16 → 32 → 64, because 64 = 2⁶. Grids such as **52** (4 × 13) or **56** (8 × 7) cannot: they need one division into an odd number of parts first (thirteenths or sevenths), after which the rest can be halved. That first division is where a large sheet is most likely to drift out of accuracy, which is one reason pre-gridded paper exists.

## Exercises

1. A design uses a 48 grid and you want cells of at least 2 cm. What is the smallest square sheet you can use? *(Answer: 48 × 2 = 96 cm, so a 100 cm sheet.)*
2. You have a 70 cm sheet. What is the largest grid that keeps cells above 1.5 cm? *(Answer: 70 ÷ 1.5 ≈ 46.7, so a 46 grid, or a 44 or 40 grid if the design needs a divisible number.)*
3. Why can a 1:2 rectangle on a 64 × 128 grid have square cells, while the same sheet on a 64 × 64 grid cannot?
